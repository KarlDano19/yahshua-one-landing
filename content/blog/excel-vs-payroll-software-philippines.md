Excel works for payroll until a government contribution rate changes mid-year, a formula gets copied wrong, or the one person who built the sheet goes on leave. The real question isn't whether spreadsheets can compute payroll, they can, it's whether your business can survive the specific ways they fail.

## When Spreadsheets Work Fine (and When They Stop)

A spreadsheet handles payroll fine for a handful of employees with simple, stable pay structures. It stops working the moment headcount crosses roughly 15 to 20 people, pay structures diversify, or more than one person needs to touch the same file without overwriting each other's work.

The practical signals that the spreadsheet stage is ending:

- Headcount has grown past what one person can sanity-check by eye each cutoff
- Employees now have different allowance, commission, or shift-differential structures that don't share one formula
- More than one branch or department feeds numbers into the same file
- Approvals and corrections happen over email or chat instead of in one system
- The person who originally built the sheet's formulas is no longer the one running it

None of these make a spreadsheet unusable overnight. They make it progressively more likely that the next error isn't caught until a government filing is already wrong.

## What Actually Breaks: The Real Failure Points

Three failure modes account for most spreadsheet payroll problems: formulas that don't update when a statutory rate changes, manual entry errors that compound across pay periods, and no audit trail showing who changed what.

**Statutory rate drift.** SSS Circular 2024-06 raised the SSS contribution rate from 14% to 15% and the Monthly Salary Credit ceiling from ₱30,000 to ₱35,000, effective January 2025, under Republic Act 11199. Any payroll sheet that still hardcodes the old rate or the old ceiling has been under-deducting and under-remitting every single pay period since, a shortfall that accrues penalties the longer it goes uncorrected. A spreadsheet doesn't know a rate changed. A person has to know, remember, and edit the formula correctly, every time any of SSS, PhilHealth, or Pag-IBIG moves.

**Manual entry and copy-paste errors.** Research into spreadsheet use across operations teams found manual data-entry mistakes and formula errors are the two most common failure types, by a wide margin, and the damage compounds the longer a bad formula runs undetected across pay periods before anyone notices the output looks wrong.

**No audit trail.** A spreadsheet doesn't log who changed a number, when, or why. When a payslip is questioned months later, or BIR asks for a breakdown of how a specific deduction was computed, "the formula was like that" isn't an answer regulators or employees accept.

## What the Data Says About Spreadsheet and Manual Payroll Errors

51% of small businesses still run payroll on spreadsheets, and another 19% use fully manual or paper-based processes, according to G2's 2025 payroll statistics review, two groups carrying most of the risk described above.

The cost shows up in measurable ways. DOSS's research into spreadsheet use found a single significant spreadsheet error costs organizations an average of $4,315 to fix, and professionals lose an average of 3.6 hours a week, about 22 full workdays a year, correcting spreadsheet mistakes. A separate survey of 1,000 small business leaders by Employment Hero found 40% had incurred penalties due to payroll issues, with incorrect wage calculations (48%), late or missed payments (38%), and incorrect tax calculations (27%) as the most frequent causes. Globally, ADP's payroll data puts the average payroll accuracy rate at 78%, meaning roughly 1 in 4 payroll runs contain an error that needs correction.

These figures are international, not Philippine-specific, spreadsheet mechanics don't change by country. What's Philippine-specific is what sits on top: SSS, PhilHealth, Pag-IBIG, and BIR each have their own rate schedules, ceilings, and filing deadlines, which means every generic spreadsheet-error risk above gets a second, compliance-shaped consequence layered on it here.

G2's same review found businesses using payroll software see 31% fewer errors, and payroll automation overall correlates with being 33% more effective, numbers that track with removing exactly the two failure points named above: manual entry and un-updated formulas.

## Excel vs. Payroll Software: Side by Side

The practical differences show up in exactly the places spreadsheets break: rate updates, audit trails, and what happens when the person who built the sheet is unavailable.

**Table: Where the two approaches actually diverge**

| | Spreadsheet | Payroll software |
|---|---|---|
| Statutory rate updates | Manual; someone has to know a rate changed and edit the formula | Applied automatically when SSS, PhilHealth, Pag-IBIG, or BIR rates change |
| Audit trail | None by default | Every change logged, with who and when |
| Single point of failure | Usually one person understands the formula chain | Role-based access; anyone trained can run it |
| Formula errors | Can run silently for months before anyone notices | Validated computation engine, not a hand-built formula chain |
| Scaling past ~20 employees | Becomes unmanageable; breaks down with headcount and complexity | Scales without a structural redesign |
| Government form generation | Manually re-typed into BIR, SSS, PhilHealth, and Pag-IBIG forms | Generated directly from the same payroll data already on file |

**When should a Philippine SMB stop using Excel for payroll?**
Once headcount passes roughly 15 to 20 employees, pay structures diversify (shift differentials, commissions, multiple allowances), or more than one person needs to manage the same payroll file, the spreadsheet model's risks start outweighing its zero cost.

**What are the biggest risks of running payroll in a spreadsheet?**
Three stand out: formulas that don't update automatically when SSS, PhilHealth, or Pag-IBIG rates change; manual data-entry and copy-paste errors that compound across pay periods; and no audit trail showing who changed a number and when.

**How much do spreadsheet errors actually cost?**

**Does payroll software handle SSS, PhilHealth, and Pag-IBIG rate changes automatically?**
